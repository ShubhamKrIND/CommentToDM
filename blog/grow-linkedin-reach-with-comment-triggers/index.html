Why LinkedIn's algorithm rewards comments (more than anything else)

LinkedIn distributes posts in waves. When you publish, your post first reaches a small slice of your followers. If that slice engages — and comments are the heaviest-weighted engagement signal — LinkedIn expands distribution to a larger group. Then larger. Then your followers' networks. Then beyond.

The critical window is the first 60–90 minutes. What happens there determines whether your post reaches 400 people or 40,000.

Here's the thing most creators miss: a comment triggers three separate engagement signals at once:

  • The comment itself (highest weight)
  • The commenter's network seeing that they commented
  • Any replies or likes on that comment (secondary signals)

One person commenting on your post can generate 3–5 distribution signals. A comment trigger that drives 50 comments in the first hour can generate 150–250 distribution signals. That's why the reach lift isn't marginal — it's multiplicative.

What is a comment trigger, exactly?

A comment trigger is a keyword that, when typed in a comment on your LinkedIn post, automatically sends a personalized DM to the commenter.

You set the keyword. You write the DM. Someone comments. They get the message — their name, the thing you promised, your link — in under 30 seconds. Automatically.

The public side: a growing comment section that tells the algorithm "people are talking about this." The private side: a personal conversation with every single person who engaged.

The "comment X to get Y" formula

Every high-performing comment trigger post follows the same structure:

  1. Lead with a genuine insight or hook — something worth engaging with. The CTA only works if people read the post first.
  2. Name the thing you're giving away — be specific. Not "a resource." A "1-page hook framework I've used for every post over 5K views."
  3. State the exact keyword — make it obvious and visual. Bold it if you want. "Comment HOOKS below."
  4. Add a commitment signal — "and I'll DM it to you directly." That "directly" matters. It signals personal delivery, not a link dump.

The best comment trigger posts feel like the CTA is a natural extension of the content — not an add-on. If the insight is strong, people want the framework behind it. The comment trigger just makes asking for it frictionless.

What to avoid

Comment triggers fail when the post itself doesn't earn the engagement. A few mistakes that kill performance:

  • Weak hook — the first line has to stop the scroll. If the opening is generic, nobody reads far enough to see the CTA.
  • Vague offer — "comment for a resource" converts badly. "Comment GUIDE for the 12-point hook framework" converts well. Be specific.
  • Wrong keyword — single, clear, easy-to-type words work better than phrases. "GUIDE" beats "send me the guide" every time.
  • No follow-through in the DM — if your post promised a framework and the DM delivers a link to your homepage, you've broken trust. Match the DM to the promise exactly.

How many comments does it take to get meaningful algo lift?

Based on data across 4,800+ accounts using CommentToDM, here's a rough benchmark:

  • 10–25 comments in the first hour — moderate lift, post reaches beyond your direct followers
  • 25–75 comments in the first hour — strong lift, significant expansion to 2nd-degree network
  • 75+ comments in the first hour — viral lift potential, LinkedIn may push the post beyond your extended network

Your audience size matters. A creator with 5,000 followers hitting 30 comments in an hour is doing better (proportionally) than a creator with 50,000 followers hitting the same number.

The key insight: the ratio of engagement to followers matters more than raw comment count. Comment triggers help because they turn passive readers into active commenters — people who would have scrolled past suddenly have a reason to type.
